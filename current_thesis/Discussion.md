## 4.1 Model performance

The catalogue-initialised network converged rapidly, with loss, AUC and F1 stabilising within the first 20–30 training epochs across every weight scheme. This behaviour is most plausibly a direct consequence of the initialisation strategy: rather than beginning from random weights, the model was seeded from, and regularised toward, the WHO catalogue baseline through an anchor regulariser [cite]. A biologically grounded starting point constrains the effective parameter search space and accelerates convergence, but the same property is double-edged. Because the architecture is a single linear layer terminating in a per-drug sigmoid, it is functionally equivalent to a weighted logical OR over catalogued variants, and it therefore saturates its representational capacity almost as quickly as it converges. It cannot, by construction, encode interaction terms, epistatic relationships, or resistance mechanisms shared between drugs. This is a ceiling imposed by design rather than a training failure, and it frames several of the limitations and future directions discussed below.

Against this constraint, the model's sensitivity was nonetheless competitive. The catalogue-weighted scheme matched or exceeded TB-Profiler for 13 of 15 drugs, and for 14 of 15 drugs sensitivity improved after training and threshold application relative to the untrained baseline. TB-Profiler is an appropriate and demanding comparator here: it is a mature, actively maintained, rule-based tool that combines the WHO catalogue with lineage-marker data and curated heuristics refined over several years [cite]. That a comparatively simple gradient-trained representation — carrying no explicit lineage information and no epistatic encoding — could match it indicates that probabilistic, weight-based representations of catalogue knowledge retain genuine predictive value beyond static look-up. The absolute gains for well-characterised drugs were modest, but the more important observation is that the framework scales with architectural complexity in a way a fixed rule set does not.

The cross-resistance results are consistent with the relationships already formalised in the expert rules. For the fluoroquinolones, TB-Profiler's sensitivity was closely matched by the WHO+ER scheme, which is expected given that expert rule 7 already encodes fluoroquinolone class cross-resistance and therefore supplied the network with the relevant variant associations [cite]. The isoniazid–ethionamide relationship behaved similarly: both drugs are affected by *inhA* mutations through their shared enoyl-ACP reductase target, a relationship captured by expert rule 8 [cite], and the augmented scheme contributed additional sensitivity for ETO specifically. The bedaquiline–clofazimine pairing, linked through *Rv0678*/*mmpL5* by expert rule 9 [cite], showed a more revealing divergence: bedaquiline sensitivity improved substantially while clofazimine remained poor. This is most plausibly a reflection of the relative scarcity of resistant clofazimine training samples rather than a failure of the cross-resistance encoding itself. Delamanid, for which TB-Profiler performed particularly poorly, improved under all three weight schemes at minimal specificity cost — an encouraging result given how little resistance data exists for this newer agent [cite]. The clearest opportunity for improvement is architectural: a hidden layer shared across drugs with linked resistance mechanisms (the fluoroquinolone class, INH–ETO, BDQ–CFZ, and DLM–pretomanid) would allow the model to represent the very heuristics the expert rules currently hard-code, and a multi-layer architecture incorporating these could realistically approach the WHO-recommended sensitivity target [cite].

The specificity reduction observed across the threshold sweeps was a deliberate consequence of a sensitivity-prioritised strategy, and its justification rests on the asymmetric clinical consequences of the two error types. **The clinical framing in this paragraph should be read as motivation rather than as a clinical recommendation, and the specific claims below should be confirmed with clinical collaborators or anchored to clinical literature before submission (per supervisor feedback, ll. 468–483).** A false negative — predicting susceptibility where resistance is in fact present — results in a patient receiving a regimen to which their infection does not respond. Historically this carried a substantial time penalty: first-line treatment runs to roughly six months, and older second-line regimens were considerably longer, though it should be noted that newer all-oral regimens such as BPaLM have reduced treatment duration for eligible MDR/RR-TB patients to approximately six months, so the older "considerably longer" framing is now partly outdated [cite] **[confirm with Rendi, per ll. 470]**. During ineffective treatment, the organism continues to replicate, and in heteroresistant infections an ineffective drug imposes selection pressure that can drive further within-host resistance acquisition [cite]. At the population level, an undetected resistant case is a potential onward transmission event, compounding the existing global burden of drug-resistant TB [cite]. A false positive — predicting resistance where the infection is susceptible — is not without cost: the patient may receive a more intensive regimen, with the attendant burden of toxicity (the fluoroquinolones, linezolid and clofazimine each carry well-documented adverse-effect profiles [cite]), expense and duration. The clinically relevant asymmetry is that a false positive places a patient on effective if over-intensive therapy, a more recoverable trajectory than the missed-resistance scenario.

Quantitatively, the model's worst-case specificity of approximately 0.80 compares with TB-Profiler's approximately 0.95. Across ten susceptible patients this amounts to roughly one additional false positive relative to TB-Profiler. Set against this, the model's higher sensitivity recovers resistant cases that TB-Profiler misses outright — and for several drugs TB-Profiler's sensitivity falls below 0.5, equivalent to missing every other resistant patient. The trade-off is therefore one additional false positive per ten susceptible patients against a substantial reduction in missed resistant cases. It should be stated explicitly that the 0.80 specificity floor was a pragmatic constraint adopted for this first iteration and was not clinically derived; the model does not currently meet the WHO standard of ≥98% sensitivity and ≥95% specificity for genotypic DST [cite], and meeting that standard is a target for subsequent development rather than a claim of the present work, which is best understood as a proof of concept for the sensitivity-boosting methodology.

The F1 score is reported for completeness as a standard classification metric, but it is not the primary interpretive lens for this clinical context, because the harmonic mean of precision and recall does not incorporate specificity and therefore gives an incomplete picture of the sensitivity–specificity trade-off that defines clinical utility here [cite]. Optimising for F1 would have obscured the deliberate asymmetry of the threshold strategy. What F1 does confirm is that the specificity cost did not uniformly degrade classification quality: for drugs with strong catalogue coverage and sufficient resistant training data, F1 matched or exceeded TB-Profiler, indicating that the sensitivity gains were achieved without disproportionate precision losses. Where F1 declined — amikacin, bedaquiline, capreomycin, kanamycin, linezolid and pyrazinamide — the pattern tracks class imbalance and data limitation rather than any fundamental modelling failure.

## 4.2 Candidate mutations — first-line drugs

Sensitivity-contributing candidate variants were recovered for three of the four first-line drugs, with isoniazid, rifampicin and ethambutol all yielding annotated candidates and pyrazinamide yielding a large filtered pool but no ablation-threshold crossers. With the exception of the confounders discussed below, the biological coherence of these candidates — their concentration in the correct target genes — constitutes strong internal validation of the pipeline.

For **isoniazid**, the leading candidate was a *katG* substitution (genome position 2155168_C>G; present in 100% of isoniazid false-negative samples; ΔSensitivity = 6.26%) **[confirm: this coordinate corresponds to the canonical *katG* codon 315 (Ser315) — verify against NC_000962.3]**. *katG* encodes the catalase-peroxidase that activates isoniazid from its prodrug form to the active isonicotinoyl-NAD adduct, and a loss-of-function variant here therefore abolishes drug activation [cite]; an uncatalogued variant at this position represents a clinically meaningful coverage gap and is the highest-priority isoniazid candidate for validation and catalogue submission. The variant carries an infinite odds ratio (rendered as a green marker), reflecting exclusive presence in resistant false-negative samples with no susceptible carriers; this is an artefact of contingency-table construction under these conditions rather than a biological anomaly, and should resolve to a finite estimate with expanded data. The second candidate, also a C>T substitution, is annotated as intergenic at genome position 1673425 (ΔSensitivity = 4.56%; FN prevalence not reported) — that is, within the *fabG1*/*inhA* (mabA-*inhA*) promoter, at the position corresponding to the canonical −15 C>T mutation that upregulates *inhA* and confers low-level resistance to both isoniazid and ethionamide [cite]. The identical variant (1673425_C>T) was independently recovered as the leading ethionamide candidate (§4.5); its appearance for both drugs at the same coordinate is precisely the cross-resistance signature this promoter mechanism predicts, making it a strong, mechanistically coherent second-priority candidate. The third candidate, the *rpoB* substitution 761155_C>T (FN prevalence 50%; ΔSensitivity = 0.73%), is flagged explicitly as a **confounder**: it is the very same variant that is the dominant rifampicin candidate above, and *rpoB* — the RNA polymerase β-subunit and rifampicin target — has no mechanistic role in isoniazid action. Its appearance reflects the co-occurrence of *rpoB* and isoniazid-resistance mutations within MDR strains, and its inclusion in an isoniazid weight matrix would risk false positives in susceptible samples carrying *rpoB* mutations for rifampicin-related reasons alone. This variant recurs across multiple drugs and is treated as a systematic confounder below.

For **rifampicin**, every annotated candidate fell within *rpoB* itself, which validates the pipeline on three counts simultaneously: the correct gene, the correct location, and the absence of off-target candidates. The dominant variant (761155_C>T; present in 97% of rifampicin false-negative samples; ΔSensitivity = 14.99%) carried a −log₁₀(p) of approximately 120 and a log₁₀(OR) exceeding 3, corresponding to more than a thousand-fold enrichment in resistant samples; it produced the largest single-variant sensitivity contribution among the first-line drugs and represents a major catalogue gap **[confirm: 761155_C>T is very likely the canonical *rpoB* Ser450Leu — verify]**. The remaining three candidates were 761110_A>T (FN prevalence 89%; ΔSensitivity = 3.37%), 761139_C>G (FN prevalence 100%; ΔSensitivity = 1.22%; infinite OR) and 761139_C>T (FN prevalence 100%; ΔSensitivity = 1.26%; infinite OR). All four fall within or immediately flank the Rifampicin Resistance Determining Region (RRDR, codons 426–452), consistent with the established molecular epidemiology of rifampicin resistance [cite], and the filtered set as a whole constitutes a strong shortlist for validation and catalogue submission.

**Ethambutol** produced the most diverse annotated candidate set of the first-line drugs. Seven *embB* candidates were recovered, five of them ablation-contributing across distinct positions and substitution types — 4247429_A>G (FN prevalence 95%; ΔSensitivity = 2.56%), 4248003_A>G (92%; 0.66%), 4247431_G>C (80%; 0.97%), 4247431_G>A (38%; 1.05%) and 4247574_A>C (100%; 1.77%) — alongside two further filtered *embB* variants (4247847_A>G, 90%; 4247730_G>C, 80%) carrying no calculable ΔSensitivity **[confirm: codon/amino-acid positions]**. *embB* encodes an arabinosyltransferase essential for cell-wall arabinogalactan biosynthesis and is the primary ethambutol target [cite]. The diversity of substitution types across multiple positions indicates a spectrum of resistance variants rather than a single dominant mutation, which is consistent with the wide range of *embB* mutations reported in clinical ethambutol-resistant isolates [cite] and implies that current catalogue coverage of this gene is incomplete. The auxiliary loci *embC* and *ubiA* also appeared in the broader filtered set. Two confounders were present here as well — the *rpoB* substitution 761155_C>T (FN prevalence 17%; ΔSensitivity = 2.33%) and the *katG* substitution 2155168_C>G (FN prevalence 11%; ΔSensitivity = 0.97%), which are the canonical rifampicin and isoniazid resistance mutations respectively and have no mechanistic role in ethambutol resistance. Both carried unusually high −log₁₀(p) values for genes unconnected to the drug, which is itself the diagnostic signature: extreme statistical significance in a mechanistically unrelated context indicates systematic confounding, most plausibly because a variant enriched in MDR strains appears enriched for every drug those strains simultaneously resist. They are flagged as probable confounders rather than ethambutol-specific determinants.

**Pyrazinamide** yielded no ablation-threshold candidates despite a large and densely populated volcano plot with many significant positive associations centred on *pncA*, the pyrazinamidase that activates the prodrug [cite]. This is best framed as a methodological observation rather than a negative result: pyrazinamide resistance follows a distributed architecture, spread across many variants in *pncA* and beyond, with no single variant contributing enough to cross the ablation threshold individually. Single-variant ablation is therefore not the appropriate tool for this drug, and pathway-level or multi-variant aggregation approaches would be better suited. It is also worth noting, as a caveat to any pyrazinamide interpretation, that phenotypic drug-susceptibility testing for pyrazinamide is notoriously unreliable [cite] (ll. 120); some of the apparent false-negative signal for this drug may reflect phenotyping error rather than genuine uncatalogued resistance, which further supports treating the filtered set as a hypothesis-generating pool for wet-lab validation rather than as a confident candidate list.

The recurrence of the *rpoB* 761155_C>T variant warrants standalone treatment, because it appears across multiple drugs — including several where *rpoB* has no resistance role — at −log₁₀(p) values reaching approximately 140. The variant is the canonical rifampicin candidate identified above (likely *rpoB* Ser450Leu), so its appearance as a high-significance candidate for isoniazid and ethambutol is biologically implausible: it is the rifampicin resistance mutation surfacing in unrelated analyses. The mechanism is co-occurrence in the MDR strain background — with the Fisher's exact test run per drug and no MDR-background control, a variant near-universal among MDR strains reaches significance for every drug those strains resist. The false-negative prevalences make this explicit: 761155_C>T is carried by 97% of rifampicin false-negatives (where it is causal) but only 50% of isoniazid and 17% of ethambutol false-negatives (where it is merely co-selected) — a gradient consistent with causation in the first case and confounding in the others. The same logic applies to *katG* 2155168_C>G, the canonical isoniazid mutation, which surfaces as a confounder for ethambutol (11% FN prevalence) and streptomycin (36%). The extreme p-values may additionally point to an anomaly in contingency-table construction worth a computational check. The practical implication is firm: systematic identification and exclusion of such MDR-background confounders — for instance by conditioning the per-drug test on MDR status — should be built into the filtering pipeline.

## 4.3 Candidate mutations — second-line Group A

Group A divides cleanly into the well-characterised fluoroquinolones, which produced well-populated plots and meaningful candidate sets, and the newer oral agents bedaquiline and linezolid, whose plots were sparse. The contrast is driven by duration of clinical use and the corresponding number of resistant samples available.

### Fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin and moxifloxacin)

Levofloxacin and moxifloxacin are treated together here, as they share a resistance mechanism and differ in this dataset essentially only in the number of phenotypes available (per supervisor, ll. 171). For both drugs, every annotated candidate fell within *gyrA*, which encodes DNA gyrase subunit A, the primary fluoroquinolone target; the complete absence of off-target gene annotations is a strong indicator of pipeline specificity. The affected positions fall within the *gyrA* quinolone-resistance-determining region (QRDR, approximately codons 88–94), the established locus of fluoroquinolone resistance [cite].

The leading candidate for both drugs was the change at genome positions 7582–7585, written here as `ACAG>GCAC` (levofloxacin: FN prevalence 100%, ΔSensitivity = 21.09%; moxifloxacin: FN prevalence 44%, ΔSensitivity = 32.55%, the largest single-variant contribution in all of Group A). An important correction to its initial description (per supervisor, ll. 158): this is **not a true multinucleotide polymorphism**. Within the four-base block, only the first and fourth positions are substituted while the two internal bases are unchanged, so the change represents two linked single-nucleotide substitutions rather than a contiguous MNP. Because these two positions sit in adjacent codons, they alter two separate amino acids — the first substitution corresponding to the canonical *gyrA* Asp94 codon and the second to the neighbouring codon **[confirm: Asp94Gly + adjacent codon 95 identity against NC_000962.3]**. The two substitutions appear to co-occur consistently across samples, which is why they were initially grouped and why it remains reasonable to discuss them as a single unit, provided the description above makes clear that they are distinct events affecting distinct residues. The scale and location of this change are consistent with substantial conformational alteration of the gyrase A subunit that would impair fluoroquinolone engagement [cite]. The recovery of the identical change for both fluoroquinolones substantially strengthens the case that it is a genuine resistance determinant rather than a statistical artefact, provides a mechanistic anchor for class cross-resistance, and makes it the highest-priority candidate for wet-lab validation across the entire second-line analysis.

The second shared candidate was 7570_C>T (levofloxacin: FN prevalence 70%, ΔSensitivity = 9.98%; moxifloxacin: FN prevalence 40%, ΔSensitivity = 11.51%) **[confirm: 7570_C>T very likely corresponds to the canonical *gyrA* Ala90Val — verify]**. Its consistent performance across both drugs, its location in the target gene, and its passage through all filters make it a strong second-priority candidate. Two further levofloxacin-only candidates — 7572_T>C (FN prevalence 100%; ΔSensitivity = 2.73%; infinite OR) and 7582_ACAG>CCAC (FN prevalence 100%; ΔSensitivity = 3.7%; infinite OR) — fell below the highest-impact tier but remain within the QRDR and are consistent with the broader picture of *gyrA*-mediated resistance. Notably, the larger ΔSensitivity values observed for moxifloxacin despite its lower false-negative prevalence are attributable to its smaller significant-variant pool, within which each variant carries proportionally greater independent predictive weight.

### Bedaquiline and linezolid

Bedaquiline yielded only four significant positive associations, a single filtered variant, and no ablation annotations. This is a data limitation rather than a modelling failure: bedaquiline was approved in 2012 and its widespread use is recent [cite], so *M. tuberculosis* populations have had limited generational exposure under bedaquiline selection pressure, and resistant isolates remain rare in global surveillance datasets — constraining both the statistical power of the Fisher's exact test and the training signal available for weight refinement. The single filtered variant is best understood as a starting point rather than a ceiling, and the WHO endorsement of the BPaLM regimen is expected to generate substantially more resistance data, which future pipeline iterations are well-positioned to exploit [cite].

Linezolid presents a mechanistically distinct picture. It produced 164 significant positive associations — so statistical signal is clearly present — but this signal is distributed across many small-effect variants, none of which crosses the ablation threshold individually. The methodological implication mirrors that for pyrazinamide and delamanid: single-variant ablation is insufficient, and pathway-level or multi-variant aggregation approaches are needed. Linezolid was repurposed from gram-positive infection and entered routine MDR-TB use around 2019 [cite], and it too is a component of BPaLM, so expanding data is anticipated. One mechanistic pointer is worth carrying forward for future analyses (per supervisor, ll. 203): in *Enterococcus* and other gram-positives, *de novo* linezolid resistance is mediated by mutations in the 23S rRNA gene, characteristically requiring mutation in at least three of the multiple rRNA gene copies, and the analogous determinants in *M. tuberculosis* are the 23S rRNA gene *rrl* and the ribosomal protein gene *rplC* [cite]. Variants mapping to these rRNA loci should be specifically watched for in subsequent iterations, as the multi-copy nature of rRNA genes may also partly explain why no single linezolid variant reaches the ablation threshold here.

### Density contrast between first- and second-line drugs

The step-down in plot density from first- to second-line drugs reflects evolutionary timeline rather than analytical failure. The first-line drugs have been in clinical use since the 1950s–60s [cite], affording roughly 60–70 years of selective pressure during which resistance mutations have accumulated not only in direct target genes but also in compensatory and distant epistatic loci; downstream validators should bear this epistatic complexity in mind. The second-line drugs are more recent and have had a correspondingly compressed window for resistance diversification, which produces sparser plots. A useful corollary is that the black-bordered second-line variants, though fewer, may represent a higher proportion of true resistance variants precisely because the mutational landscape is less saturated. The annotated candidates are particularly compelling on this view: TB-Profiler — a sophisticated tool combining catalogue, lineage and heuristic information — failed to classify the source samples, and from those missed samples the pipeline recovered uncatalogued variants in established drug-target genes that passed statistical filtering, survived lineage exclusion, were absent from existing catalogues, and improved sensitivity. The convergence of statistical, functional and biological evidence makes a strong case for prioritised validation.

## 4.5 Candidate mutations — second-line Group C

### Delamanid

Delamanid produced 320 significant positive associations, of which roughly 18 passed full filtering, but none crossed the ablation threshold — the same distributed-resistance pattern seen for pyrazinamide and linezolid rather than a data limitation. Delamanid is a nitroimidazole prodrug activated through the F₄₂₀ coenzyme pathway, after which unstable intermediates generate reactive oxygen species that simultaneously inhibit cell-wall synthesis and disrupt respiration [cite]. Resistance arises through loss-of-function mutations distributed across the F₄₂₀ biosynthesis and utilisation pathway — *ddn*, *fbiA*, *fbiB*, *fbiC*, *fgd1* and *Rv2983* — and the absence of a single dominant target gene is precisely why no individual variant reaches the ablation threshold [cite]. This distributed architecture has a useful corollary: because delamanid and pretomanid share the same F₄₂₀ activation pathway and accumulate loss-of-function resistance at the same loci [cite], and because pretomanid is absent from this dataset, the delamanid filtered candidate set serves as a proxy for pretomanid resistance. The roughly 18 filtered variants therefore carry implications for both drugs, which is significant given pretomanid's central role in BPaLM.

### Ethionamide

Ethionamide produced the most densely populated upper-right quadrant of any second-line drug. Its first annotated candidate was an *inhA* substitution (genome position 1674481_T>G; present in 100% of ethionamide false-negative samples; ΔSensitivity = 0.65%; infinite OR) **[confirm: codon position]**. *inhA* encodes the enoyl-ACP reductase that is the shared molecular target of both isoniazid and ethionamide. The recovery of an *inhA* variant for ethionamide is mechanistically coherent and consistent with the cross-resistance relationship formalised in expert rule 8 [cite]: although the ΔSensitivity is modest, its location in an established cross-resistance pathway makes it biologically meaningful, and its infinite odds ratio again reflects exclusive presence in resistant false-negative samples pending expanded data.

The second candidate is the most striking single finding in Group C: a C>T variant with ΔSensitivity = 25.69%, the largest sensitivity contribution among all Group C candidates and one of the largest in the entire experiment. Initially unannotated, it has resolved to an intergenic position at genome coordinate 1673425 (C>T; FN prevalence not reported), within the *fabG1*/*inhA* (mabA-*inhA*) promoter at the canonical −15 position. This confirms the suggestion (raised by the supervisor, ll. 329) that the variant is a promoter mutation rather than an unknown locus: mutations here are well established as driving *inhA* overexpression and conferring resistance to both isoniazid and ethionamide [cite]. Decisively, the isoniazid candidate set independently returned the identical coordinate (1673425_C>T; §4.2) — the same variant surfacing for both drugs is in-data confirmation of the shared cross-resistance mechanism, not merely a parallel inference. The result is doubly informative. Mechanistically, it places the largest Group C signal squarely within the canonical isoniazid–ethionamide cross-resistance pathway. Methodologically, it is a clear validation of the false-negative augmentation approach: because promoter mutations were deliberately excluded from the gene-based catalogue, this is a genuine, mechanism-backed resistance determinant that the catalogue omits by design, yet the pipeline recovered it from samples the catalogue-based tool had misclassified. It is therefore both a priority for wet-lab validation and a concrete argument for revisiting the catalogue's exclusion of promoter mutations.

### Amikacin

Amikacin yielded a single annotated candidate of high quality: an *rrs* substitution (genome position 1473246_A>G; present in 100% of amikacin false-negative samples; ΔSensitivity = 25.5%; infinite OR) corresponding to the well-characterised A1401G mutation **[confirm: A1401G assignment]**. *rrs* encodes the 16S ribosomal RNA that forms the structural and functional core of the 30S ribosomal subunit and is the primary target of the aminoglycosides; the A1401G substitution distorts the aminoglycoside binding site, prevents drug binding, and confers high-level resistance [cite]. Because amikacin, kanamycin and capreomycin share this ribosomal binding site, A1401G abolishes binding of all three simultaneously [cite]; consistent with this, the identical variant (1473246_A>G) was the leading candidate for capreomycin (FN prevalence 90%; ΔSensitivity = 30.25%) and kanamycin (100%; ΔSensitivity = 14.32%), so the same substitution independently tops the candidate list for all three injectables — strong cross-drug corroboration of a single determinant. Despite the infinite odds ratio — again a reflection of dataset size rather than biological anomaly — the convergence of an established target gene, a 25.5% sensitivity contribution and documented cross-resistance makes this one of the most compelling individual findings in the study and among the highest priorities for wet-lab validation.

## 4.6 PE/PPE family variants as candidate resistance and compensatory loci

> **Structural note (per supervisor, ll. 758): this section foregrounds the data and keeps the supporting literature deliberately brief. Detailed background on PE/PPE biology — the family's size, nomenclature, and roles in virulence and immune evasion — belongs in the Introduction rather than here, and the references below are intended only to anchor interpretation, not to introduce a new body of literature in the Discussion. Where the gene-by-gene treatment still risks reading as a mini-review, consider relocating the mechanistic detail to the background and retaining only the data and the one-line functional anchor in this section.**

Gene annotation of the filtered candidates revealed PE/PPE family variants across 12 of the 16 drugs evaluated, spanning pharmacologically distinct classes with different targets and resistance pathways. This breadth and consistency argue against incidental noise and in favour of a systematic biological phenomenon, which is the central observation this section interprets. The PE/PPE family — named for its conserved Pro-Glu and Pro-Pro-Glu N-terminal motifs and comprising a substantial fraction of the *M. tuberculosis* coding genome — is among the least functionally characterised regions of the genome [cite], which is precisely why a recurrent association with uncatalogued resistance is worth examining rather than dismissing.

The most informative individual observation is the behaviour of *PPE54* variant 3733405_G>A, which recurs across the three first-line drugs that share an MDR background. It is present in 73% of isoniazid false-negative samples (with only 4% of resistant samples lacking it), 73% of rifampicin false-negative samples (2% of resistant samples lacking it), and 75% of ethambutol false-negative samples (2% lacking it). *PPE54* is documented as a compensatory partner that partially restores the fitness cost of *katG* loss-of-function and as a participant in epistatic interactions with *rpoB* and *embB* that secure high-level resistance [cite]; its near-universal presence across the resistant population is most consistent with a compensatory or resistance-modulating role rather than a primary determinant. Critically, the prevalence patterns are not identical across the three drugs, which is the feature that distinguishes a genuine drug-associated signal from a uniform MDR-background confounder: a simple confounder would appear at comparable frequencies across unrelated drugs, whereas these drug-specific differences suggest enrichment that is at least partly mechanism-linked. A second *PPE54* variant, 3733476_A>AG, present in 37% of isoniazid false-negatives, supports the same interpretation.

Several other PE/PPE loci show comparably high false-negative prevalence and map to genes with plausible envelope, efflux or transport functions. *PPE51*, implicated in nutrient acquisition and multi-drug susceptibility and in upregulation of inner-membrane efflux [cite], appears as 3502814_G>A in 100% of ethionamide false-negatives (5% of resistant samples lacking it) and as 3502457_G>A in 58% of streptomycin false-negatives. *PPE50*, an interacting partner of *PPE51* in outer-membrane transport [cite], appears as 3501668_G>C in 100% of ciprofloxacin false-negatives (carried by 87% of resistant samples). *PPE55*, reported as an alternative pyrazinamide-resistance pathway active in strains with wild-type *pncA* [cite], appears as 3752065_A>C in 100% of pyrazinamide false-negatives (3% of resistant samples lacking it) and as 3752419_C>G in 62%, with additional variants in 39–65% of ethionamide false-negatives that are not explained by its documented pyrazinamide biology and so warrant separate investigation. *PPE60* contributes a shared deletion, 3894503_GGCC>G, present at 89% prevalence across ethambutol, isoniazid and rifampicin false-negatives — a pattern that, given its documented association is with kanamycin rather than the first-line drugs [cite], more likely reflects either a broad envelope-modifying role or MDR-strain enrichment.

A related set of PE_PGRS variants connects to the more extensively drug-resistant strain backgrounds. The clearest cautionary example is *PE_PGRS6* variant 624157_T>G, which appears in isoniazid, rifampicin and ethambutol false-negatives at 88% prevalence in each, with only 2% of resistant samples lacking it across all three. The identical variant at identical prevalence across three first-line drugs is most parsimoniously a fixed or near-fixed feature of the MDR strain background in this cohort rather than a drug-specific determinant; it is nonetheless a useful genomic marker of the uncatalogued MDR population and retains diagnostic utility even if its role is compensatory. Other PE_PGRS loci — *PE_PGRS4* (multiple structural variants in 59–74% of ciprofloxacin false-negatives, consistent with reported XDR-TB enrichment [cite]), *PE_PGRS7*, *PE_PGRS9* (a non-canonical pyrazinamide-resistance association mirroring *PPE55* [cite]), *PE_PGRS19* and *PE_PGRS33* — show patterns that are biologically suggestive but more variable, and are best treated as indirect, microenvironment-modulating associations rather than direct drug-target interactions.

Taken together, three conclusions follow. First, the PE/PPE associations are not incidental: their pervasiveness across 12 drug classes, their overlap with documented epistatic interactions, and their high false-negative prevalence collectively argue for systematic biological relevance. Second, the most parsimonious interpretation for the majority is that of compensatory mutations or resistance-amplifying modifiers — they appear alongside canonical resistance genes, occur at high prevalence across resistant populations, and map to genes governing envelope architecture, efflux regulation and intracellular survival, rather than modifying drug targets directly. Third, the highest-priority candidates for functional characterisation are those whose prevalence approaches universality in the false-negative population while being almost absent from the resistant complement: *PPE54* 3733405_G>A (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol), *PPE51* 3502814_G>A (ethionamide), *PPE55* 3752065_A>C (pyrazinamide), and *PE_PGRS6* 624157_T>G (the candidate MDR marker). These findings also motivate a concrete architectural extension: a dedicated PE/PPE input channel connected through a shared hidden layer with canonical resistance-gene features would allow the model to capture the compensatory and epistatic relationships the current single-layer architecture cannot encode, and would combine naturally with the cross-resistance hidden layer proposed in §4.1.

## 4.7 Limitations

The most consequential limitation is the scarcity of resistant cases for the newer agents. Class imbalance affects bedaquiline, linezolid, delamanid and clofazimine, and no resistance data at all was available for terizidone, pretomanid or prothionamide; a model cannot learn what it has not observed, and several of the per-drug findings above are constrained accordingly. The architecture imposes a second, related limitation: the model converges rapidly and therefore reaches its representational ceiling early. A single linear layer behaves as a weighted logical OR over catalogued variants and cannot, in principle, learn cross-resistance, pleiotropy or epistatic interaction — the isoniazid–ethionamide relationship via *inhA* being the clearest example of a biologically established mechanism that cannot be represented without shared hidden layers.

The filtering procedure also requires strengthening. The recurrence of MDR-background confounders such as the *rpoB* C>T variant across mechanistically unrelated drugs shows that confounder identification and removal need to be systematic rather than post-hoc, and the per-drug Fisher's exact test should incorporate a control for MDR strain background — for example by including MDR status as a covariate or by using a conditional test — to prevent any variant enriched in MDR strains from reaching spurious significance across the drugs those strains resist. More comprehensive lineage-pattern screening is needed for the same reason. The significance threshold itself may be miscalibrated for the data-poor drugs: a uniform −log₁₀(p) ≥ 5 cut-off may exclude genuine variants for drugs with few resistant samples, and drug-specific thresholds may be more appropriate. A further issue, now resolved, concerned gene annotation: variants lacking a human-readable gene symbol were initially at risk of being dropped from the output, but these positions are now reported either as intergenic regions or by their locus tag — and the fact that one proved to be the high-impact *fabG1* promoter variant (§4.5) underscores why preserving them mattered.

## 4.8 Future directions

Several lines of work follow directly. The most immediate is dataset expansion for the newer agents — phenotypic resistance data for terizidone, pretomanid and prothionamide, and substantially more resistance data for bedaquiline, linezolid, delamanid and clofazimine — for which the rollout of the BPaLM regimen is a promising source [cite]. External validation should follow, drawing on independent cohorts such as the CRyPTIC consortium and MIRU-VNTR–typed collections [cite], together with lineage-stratified evaluation, which the present analysis did not perform and which is necessary to support any claim of generalisability across *M. tuberculosis* lineages.

On the modelling side, the two architectural extensions motivated above — hidden layers shared across drugs with linked resistance mechanisms (the fluoroquinolone class, isoniazid–ethionamide, bedaquiline–clofazimine and delamanid–pretomanid), and a dedicated PE/PPE feature channel — together constitute the primary direction for the next iteration, as they would allow the model to represent precisely the cross-resistance and compensatory relationships the current architecture cannot. The pipeline itself should be hardened in parallel by building systematic MDR-background and lineage-confounder screening — including a conditional Fisher's test — into the filtering stage.

Finally, the strongest candidates warrant prioritised wet-lab validation, with a view to eventual inclusion in future catalogues or AMR-prediction tools if confirmed. In approximate priority order these are: the *gyrA* change at positions 7582–7585 (shared by levofloxacin and moxifloxacin, the largest effects and the most biologically corroborated); the *rrs* A1401G substitution 1473246_A>G, which independently tops the candidate list for all three injectables (amikacin 25.5%, capreomycin 30.25%, kanamycin 14.32%); the *katG* candidate for isoniazid (the prodrug activator, 2155168_C>G, 6.26%); the high-prevalence *PPE54*, *PPE51* and *PPE55* variants identified in §4.6; and the *fabG1*/*inhA* promoter variant 1673425_C>T (25.69% for ethionamide, the identical coordinate also recovered for isoniazid), strongly motivated for validation given its established role in isoniazid–ethionamide cross-resistance. A natural supplementary analysis would extend the cross-resistance examination to the unclassified agents already in the dataset — ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, capreomycin, kanamycin and streptomycin — which surfaced canonical determinants of their own (for example *gyrA* for the fluoroquinolones and *rpsL* for streptomycin) and carry direct cross-resistance implications for the main drug panel.
